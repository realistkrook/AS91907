Section 03

Where you meet it

03 — Applications

Most encounters with artificial intelligence are unremarkable. It is not a robot or an assistant; it is a ranking step inside an app you were already using.

The examples below were chosen because they are ordinary. Each one also shows a different shape of the same underlying idea described in section 01: a system trained on examples, producing a prediction.

Recommendation systems

This is almost certainly the AI you use most. Every ordered feed — YouTube’s suggestions, Spotify’s weekly playlist, a shopping site’s “you might also like” — is a recommendation system deciding what to show and in what order.

The common technique is collaborative filtering, and the principle is simple: find people whose past behaviour resembles yours, then suggest things they liked which you have not encountered. The system needs no understanding of the music or the film. It only needs the pattern of what tends to be enjoyed together.

Why recommendations narrow over time

A recommender is optimised for engagement, not for breadth. If you watch one video on a topic, similar videos are promoted, which makes you more likely to watch another, which promotes more of the same.

The loop is not a malfunction — the system is doing exactly what it was built to do. Whether that is what the user wanted is a separate question, taken up in section 04.

Chatbots and language models

Language models generate text by repeatedly predicting the next word. Their practical strength is anything where fluent, plausible language is the actual goal: drafting, summarising, rewording, translating, explaining code.

Their weakness follows from the same mechanism. A model produces what reads correctly, which is not the same as what is correct. When it states a false fact in confident prose, this is often called a hallucination — a misleading term, since it implies an unusual failure. Producing plausible text is the normal operation; sometimes the plausible text happens to be true.

A language model is a very good writer and an unreliable witness. The two are not in tension — they are the same property.

Computer vision

Computer vision covers anything that interprets images: unlocking a phone by face, reading a licence plate, flagging a suspicious region on a medical scan, sorting recycling on a conveyor.

Medical imaging is the case worth dwelling on, because it shows where these systems fit best. A model trained on a large archive of scans can flag areas for review faster and more consistently than a tired human. It does not diagnose. It narrows what a radiologist looks at first. The pattern — AI as a filter in front of a human decision, rather than a replacement for it — is the most common successful deployment.

Driver assistance

“Self-driving car” describes a spectrum rather than a single capability. Features already common in new cars — lane keeping, adaptive cruise control, automatic emergency braking — use computer vision and sensors to assist a driver who stays responsible.

Full autonomy, with no human attention required in any conditions, has proved considerably harder than expected. The reason is instructive: driving is mostly routine, and machine learning is good at routine. The difficulty is the rare case — an unusual obstacle, a hand signal from a police officer, unfamiliar roadworks. Rare cases are by definition scarce in training data, and it is precisely in rare cases that the cost of being wrong is highest.

What the examples have in common

Applications compared by task and consequence of error
Application Underlying task Cost of being wrong
Recommendations Rank items by predicted interest Low — a poor suggestion
Language models Predict the next word Varies — depends on whether anyone checks
Medical imaging Classify regions of an image High — mitigated by human review
Driver assistance Detect objects, predict motion Severe — and immediate

Every row is a prediction learned from examples. What differs is the consequence of a wrong prediction, and how much human judgement sits between the output and the outcome. That, rather than technical sophistication, is usually what determines whether a deployment is sensible.
