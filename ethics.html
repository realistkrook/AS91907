Section 04

The harder questions

04 — Ethics

The debate about AI tends to be conducted at two unhelpful extremes: distant speculation about machines that overthrow humanity, and flat denial that anything has changed. The problems worth attention are less dramatic than the first and considerably more real than the second.

Four are set out below. None has a settled answer. Where reasonable people disagree, this page says so rather than presenting one position as concluded.

Bias

A system trained on examples inherits whatever patterns those examples contain, including patterns nobody intended it to learn. If a company’s past hiring favoured one group, a model trained on that hiring history will treat the characteristics of that group as indicators of a good candidate.

The mechanism deserves care. The model is not prejudiced; it has no beliefs. It has correctly identified a pattern in real data. The problem is that the pattern encoded a past injustice, and applying it forward repeats that injustice at scale — now with the appearance of mathematical objectivity.

An unfair decision made by a person can be questioned. The same decision made by a system is often treated as a measurement.

Removing the obvious attribute rarely fixes this. Delete gender from the data and a model may still infer it from a sports club, a school, or a gap in employment. These stand-ins are called proxy variables, and finding them is difficult precisely because nobody chose them.

Where the disagreement lies

There is broad agreement that biased outputs are a genuine problem. There is no agreement on what fairness should mean in practice.

Equal acceptance rates across groups, equal error rates, and equal treatment of similar individuals are all defensible definitions — and it has been shown that they cannot generally all be satisfied at once. Choosing between them is an ethical decision, not a technical one, and it is frequently made by default rather than deliberately.

Privacy

Machine learning needs data, and the most valuable data is often about people. Two distinct concerns get run together here.

The first is collection: what was gathered, whether consent was meaningful, and whether “agreeing” to an unread policy in order to use a service counts as a choice.

The second is inference, and it is the less obvious of the two. Systems can derive information you never supplied. Purchase timing can suggest a pregnancy; typing patterns can indicate a neurological condition; location history reveals religious observance. None of this was disclosed. It was reconstructed — which means consent to collection is not the same as consent to what can be worked out afterwards.

Work

Automation has displaced work before, and the pattern has usually been substitution of tasks rather than wholesale elimination of jobs. Cash machines did not end bank employment; they changed what branch staff did.

What may differ this time is the target. Earlier automation mostly affected physical and routine work. Current systems are strongest at tasks involving language and pattern recognition — drafting, summarising, first-pass translation, routine code, initial customer contact. That is a large share of entry-level professional work, which is also how people have traditionally gained the experience to do senior work.

The honest position is that the scale and speed are genuinely uncertain. Confident predictions in either direction — mass unemployment, or simply more jobs than before — are running ahead of the evidence.

Misinformation

Convincing fake text, images, audio and video can now be produced cheaply and at volume. Fabrication is not new; the change is in cost and quantity.

The most-discussed risk is people being deceived by a fake. The more corrosive one may be the reverse: once anything could be fabricated, genuine evidence becomes deniable. A recording of real wrongdoing can simply be dismissed as synthetic. The damage is done not by any particular forgery but by the general erosion of the assumption that evidence means something.

Who is responsible?

A recurring difficulty runs through all four problems. When an automated decision causes harm, responsibility is unusually easy to disperse.

The engineers built a general tool. The company deployed it within its stated limits. The operator followed the recommendation. The model cannot be held responsible in any meaningful sense. Everyone acted reasonably given their own position, and the harm still occurred.

This is sometimes called the accountability gap, and it is arguably the central ethical question, because it determines whether any of the others can actually be remedied. A harm nobody is answerable for is a harm that tends to persist.

Questions worth carrying forward

When a system makes a decision about you, are you entitled to an explanation? To appeal it to a person?

Should the people whose work trained a model have a claim on what it produces?

Who should decide which definition of fairness a system is built around — and should that choice be published?
