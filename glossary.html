Glossary

About

Every term defined here is one this guide actually uses. Each entry links back to the section where the term appears in context, since a definition in isolation is rarely as useful as seeing the idea put to work.

Much of this vocabulary — learning, training, neural, intelligence, hallucination — is borrowed from human cognition and consistently oversells what the systems are doing. It is useful shorthand, but it is shorthand.

16 terms

Accountability gap
The difficulty of identifying who is answerable when an automated decision causes harm. Responsibility disperses across the people who built the system, those who deployed it and those who acted on its output — each of whom acted reasonably in their own role. Introduced in Section 04 →
Algorithm
A fixed sequence of steps for solving a problem. A recipe is an algorithm, and so is long division. Ordinary software is built almost entirely from algorithms a person wrote out explicitly. Introduced in Section 01 →
Collaborative filtering
The common technique behind recommendation systems. It finds people whose past behaviour resembles yours and suggests things they liked, without needing any understanding of the items themselves. Introduced in Section 03 →
Computer vision
Any system that interprets images — recognising a face, reading a licence plate, flagging a region on a medical scan, sorting objects on a conveyor. Introduced in Section 03 →
Deep learning
Machine learning using neural networks with many layers. The word “deep” refers to the number of layers and nothing more mysterious than that. Introduced in Section 01 →
Hallucination
The usual term for a language model stating something false in confident prose. The word is misleading: producing plausible text is the system’s normal operation, and sometimes the plausible text happens to be true. Introduced in Section 03 →
Large language model
A neural network trained on a very large quantity of text to predict which word comes next. Repeating that prediction and feeding each new word back in produces paragraphs. Introduced in Section 01 →
Machine learning
Deriving rules from worked examples rather than being given them. It exists because there is a large class of problems humans can solve but cannot explain clearly enough to write down. Introduced in Section 01 →
Neural network
Layers of simple units that each multiply incoming numbers by their own weights and pass the total onward. Loosely — very loosely — inspired by neurons in a brain. Introduced in Section 01 →
Proxy variable
A piece of data that stands in for another, usually unintentionally. Removing gender from a dataset does not remove it if a sports club, a school or an employment gap still implies it. Introduced in Section 04 →
Reinforcement learning
Training in which a system is given a goal and a score after each attempt, rather than labelled examples. Used for game-playing systems and robot control. Introduced in Section 01 →
Supervised learning
Training on examples that already carry the correct answer. Spam filters and medical image screening are typical uses. Introduced in Section 01 →
Training data
The collection of examples a system learns from. Its contents matter enormously: a model inherits whatever patterns the examples contain, including ones nobody intended it to learn. Introduced in Section 01 →
Transformer
The neural network architecture introduced in 2017 that processes all words in a passage in parallel rather than one at a time. It underlies essentially every large language model in current use. Introduced in Section 02 →
Unsupervised learning
Training on data with no answers attached, leaving the system to find structure itself — for example grouping customers by buying patterns. Introduced in Section 01 →
Weights
The adjustable numbers inside a neural network. Training means nudging them repeatedly until the network’s outputs match the expected answers more often. A large model may have billions. Introduced in Section 01 →
