Section 02

A short history

02 — Timeline

Artificial intelligence is older than most people assume, and its progress has been far less steady. Two periods of collapsed funding — both following confident predictions — are as much a part of the story as the breakthroughs.

Fourteen moments are set out below. The pattern worth watching is the swing between two ideas: writing down rules for a machine to follow, and letting a machine derive its own rules from examples.

Photographs of three of these machines are in the gallery.

Timeline

Founding ideas1950 – 1969

The field’s central questions were posed before there was hardware capable of answering them.

1950

Turing asks the question differently

In Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Alan Turing sets aside “can machines think?” as too vague and proposes a practical substitute: could a machine hold a written conversation indistinguishable from a person’s? The move — judging behaviour rather than defining thought — still shapes how systems are evaluated.

1956

The field is named

A summer workshop at Dartmouth College gathers researchers around John McCarthy’s proposal, which introduces the term artificial intelligence. The organisers expected significant progress over a single summer. This turned out to be optimistic by several decades.

1958

The perceptron

Frank Rosenblatt builds a system that adjusts its own weights in response to being told whether it was right. It is the direct ancestor of every neural network described in section 01, and the first working demonstration that a machine could learn from examples rather than rules.

1966

ELIZA, and a lesson about people

Joseph Weizenbaum’s program imitates a therapist by matching patterns and reflecting sentences back. It understands nothing. Weizenbaum was disturbed by how readily users confided in it — an early sign that people attribute understanding to systems that merely produce fluent language.

The first winter1969 – 1980

Early promises met their limits, and the funding that had followed the promises withdrew.

1969

Perceptrons and their limits

Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert prove that a single-layer perceptron cannot learn even simple functions such as exclusive-or. The mathematics was correct, and multi-layer networks would later resolve it — but the result redirected attention and money away from neural approaches for years.

1973

The Lighthill report

A review commissioned by the UK government concludes that AI research had failed to deliver on its claims. British funding is cut sharply. The pattern — ambitious promise, disappointing delivery, abrupt withdrawal of support — is now familiar enough to have a name.

Expert systems1980 – 1993

A commercially successful detour: encode a specialist’s knowledge as explicit rules.

1980

XCON goes into production

Digital Equipment Corporation deploys a rule-based system that configures computer orders, reportedly saving the company millions annually. Expert systems work by encoding human expertise as explicit if–then rules — the opposite of learning from examples.

1987

The second winter

The market for specialised AI hardware collapses. Expert systems prove expensive to maintain: every rule is hand-written, and the rules interact in ways that become unmanageable as they multiply. The approach does not scale to problems where nobody can state the rules.

The statistical turn1997 – 2011

Attention shifts from encoding knowledge to computing over data.

1997

Deep Blue beats Kasparov

IBM’s chess machine defeats the reigning world champion. Notably it does not learn: it searches enormous numbers of positions and scores them using rules written by people. The victory is a milestone for computation rather than for machine learning.

2006

Deep networks become trainable

Geoffrey Hinton and colleagues publish methods for training networks with many layers, which had previously been impractical. Combined with faster hardware and far larger datasets, this sets up the decade that follows.

Deep learning2012 – present

Three ingredients converge: layered networks, graphics hardware, and data at internet scale.

2012

AlexNet wins ImageNet

A deep neural network trained on graphics cards wins an image-recognition competition by a margin large enough to end the argument. Within a few years, nearly all serious computer-vision work is built this way.

2016

AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol

Go had been considered resistant to computer search because its board is far too large to enumerate. A system combining neural networks with selective search wins four games out of five — and plays moves that professional commentators initially judged to be mistakes.

2017

The transformer architecture

A paper titled Attention Is All You Need introduces a design that processes all words in a passage in parallel rather than one at a time. It is the architecture underneath essentially every large language model in use today.

2022

Language models reach everyone

The public release of ChatGPT puts a large language model in front of a general audience for the first time. The underlying technique — predicting the next word, described in section 01 — was not new. Access to it was.

Pattern

What the timeline shows

Both winters followed the same sequence: a genuine advance, a prediction that it would generalise, and a withdrawal of support when it did not. The advances were real. The forecasts built on them were not.

Every winter was preceded by confident predictions from people who had just been right about something else.

That is worth holding onto when reading current claims about where the technology goes next — a point taken up in section 04.
