Section 01

What is AI?

Definitions

“Artificial intelligence” is a label attached to a very wide range of software, much of which has little in common. It is worth separating what the phrase suggests from what the systems actually do.

The broadest useful definition is this: AI is software that performs tasks which normally require human judgement. Recognising a face, translating a sentence, recommending a film and deciding whether a transaction looks fraudulent are all examples. What counts as AI has always shifted. Optical character recognition was considered artificial intelligence in the 1970s; today it is just the thing your phone does when it reads a receipt.

That drift is worth noticing. It means AI is partly a moving target, defined as much by what still feels impressive as by any fixed technical property.

Algorithms, and why they are not the interesting part

An algorithm is simply a fixed sequence of steps for solving a problem. A recipe is an algorithm. So is the method you were taught for long division. Ordinary software is built almost entirely from algorithms that a person wrote out explicitly: if this, then that.

This works well when the rules can actually be written down. Payroll software, a calculator, a booking system — these are all rule-based and none of them are AI. The rules are known in advance, and a programmer can state them.

The difficulty arrives with problems where nobody can state the rules. Consider writing instructions to tell a cat from a dog in a photograph. You recognise the difference instantly, but try to put it into steps precise enough for a computer and the task collapses. Ear shape? Some dogs have pointed ears. Size? You cannot tell size from a photo. Whiskers? Both have them.

Machine learning exists because there is a large class of problems humans can solve but cannot explain.

Machine learning: examples instead of rules

Machine learning inverts the usual approach. Rather than writing the rules, you supply a large number of worked examples and let the system derive the rules itself.

To build a cat-and-dog classifier, you assemble many thousands of photographs, each already labelled “cat” or “dog”. This labelled collection is the training data. The system makes a guess at each image, is told whether it was right, and adjusts itself slightly in whichever direction would have produced a better answer. Repeat this several million times and the adjustments accumulate into something that classifies new, unseen photographs fairly reliably.

A caution about the word "learning"

Nothing here resembles learning in the human sense. There is no understanding of cats, no concept of an animal, no curiosity. What is happening is statistical: numbers inside the system are nudged repeatedly until its outputs match the labels more often.

The vocabulary of the field — learning, training, neural, intelligence, hallucination — is borrowed from human cognition and consistently oversells what is going on. It is useful shorthand, but it is shorthand.

Neural networks

A neural network is the structure most modern machine learning uses. It is loosely — very loosely — inspired by neurons in a brain.

Picture a large set of simple units arranged in layers. Each unit receives numbers, multiplies them by its own set of weights, adds the results together, and passes the total to the next layer. The first layer receives the raw input, such as the brightness of each pixel. The last layer produces the answer. Training means adjusting those weights, of which a large model may have billions.

The word “deep” in deep learning refers to nothing more mysterious than having many layers.

Three kinds of learning

Almost everything described as AI falls into one of three training approaches, distinguished by what the system is given.

Three approaches to machine learning
Approach What it is given Typical use
Supervised Examples with correct answers attached Spam filters, medical image screening
Unsupervised Data with no answers; it finds structure itself Grouping customers by buying patterns
Reinforcement A goal, and a score after each attempt Game-playing systems, robot control

What about ChatGPT?

Large language models are the systems most people now mean by “AI”. They are neural networks trained on an enormous quantity of text, and the task they are trained on is narrower than most people assume: predict which word comes next.

Given “the capital of France is”, the model produces a probability for every word it knows, and “Paris” scores highest. Do that repeatedly, feeding each new word back in, and paragraphs emerge.

This explains a great deal about how these systems behave. They are fluent because fluency is precisely what they optimise for. They state false things confidently because nothing in next-word prediction distinguishes true statements from plausible ones. And they have no way to check a claim against the world, because they were never connected to it — only to text describing it.

Terms introduced in this section

Algorithm — a fixed sequence of steps for solving a problem.
Machine learning — deriving rules from examples rather than being given them.
Training data — the labelled examples a system learns from.
Neural network — layered units that transform numbers, used for most modern machine learning.
Weights — the adjustable numbers inside a network that training modifies.
Deep learning — machine learning using networks with many layers.
